# Getting it back afterwards

This file is why "pay and leave" is usually the right advice. A lot of money
taken this way is recoverable, and none of the recovery methods require standing
in the street arguing.

Recovery odds, roughly:

| Paid by | Odds |
|---------|------|
| Ride-hail app | High — routine, in-app, often automatic |
| Credit card | Good — formal chargeback rights |
| Debit card | Moderate — weaker protections, slower |
| Booking platform | Moderate — depends on the platform's window |
| Cash | Near zero |

That table is also the pre-trip advice: **card for anything above a street
snack, cash only for small transactions you can afford to lose.**

---

## Ride-hail apps

The fastest win available. Report inside the app within 24–48 hours: fare
disputes, route deviations, a driver who demanded cash on top of the app fare,
or a ride that didn't match the booking. Attach screenshots of the route and the
receipt. Refunds are frequently granted without a human ever reading the case,
because the platform already has the GPS trace and it's cheaper to refund than
to argue.

Drivers demanding cash beyond the app fare is a reportable offense that gets
them deactivated — say so, in the moment, if it's safe to. Uber has a dedicated
cancellation reason for it, "the driver requested payment outside the app," which
also waives the cancellation fee; use it *before* pickup rather than negotiating.

**Never let a trip in progress be cancelled.** A driver offering to cancel and
drive you anyway for cash is removing every protection at once — the GPS trace,
the fare record, the dispute route, the insurance, and the record that this driver
ever carried you. The refund path in this section exists only for trips that stayed
on the platform.

Report wording that works: the amount, the demand, and the fact that nothing was
paid off-platform. *"The driver demanded EGP 200 in cash on top of the app fare and
refused to complete the trip at the quoted price. I did not pay anything outside
the app."* Facts and figures get processed; complaints get acknowledged.

## Card chargebacks

Card networks let you dispute a charge that was fraudulent, materially not as
described, or an amount you didn't authorize. The relevant grounds here:

- **Amount altered after you signed** — a tip line filled in, digits added.
- **Services not as described** — the tour didn't exist, the room wasn't the room.
- **Unauthorized** — the terminal was run twice, or run for a different amount
  than the one shown.
- **Dynamic currency conversion you didn't consent to** — charged in your home
  currency without being offered the choice.

Practicalities: dispute windows are typically 60–120 days from the statement,
but file within days, not weeks. Contact the issuing bank, not the merchant.
Evidence that wins: a photograph of the menu or posted price, the receipt, the
booking confirmation, and a short factual timeline. Do not editorialize — banks
respond to "the menu shows €6 per 100g, I was billed €240 for one portion", not
to how it felt.

Keep every receipt until you're home. Photograph the terminal screen if the
amount looks wrong before you tap.

## Booking platforms

Booking.com, Airbnb, GetYourGuide, Viator, Klook, Expedia all have dispute
processes, and all of them apply real pressure on a property that wants to keep
its listing. Call from the lobby while the problem is happening — platform
support intervening live works far better than a complaint filed afterwards.

Airbnb requires reporting within 24–72 hours of check-in for most claims. Check
the specific window; missing it is the usual reason claims fail.

## Tourist police

Many tourism-dependent countries run a dedicated tourist police force that
actually intervenes — Egypt, Thailand, Greece, Turkey, Malaysia, India, Peru,
Italy among them. They exist because tourism revenue is a national interest, and
they resolve overcharging disputes far more often than travelers expect,
sometimes with the vendor refunding on the spot.

**Search the current number** — `<country> tourist police number` — rather than
trusting any list, including this one. Numbers change, and a wrong emergency
number is worse than none. What's stable is the concept: ask a hotel, a tourism
office, or a uniformed officer, and expect the country to have one.

Worth knowing: in many of these countries the *threat* of involving them is more
useful than the involvement, which is why it's rung 4 of the ladder in
`scripts.md`.

## The hotel front desk

Consistently underrated. Staff know the real local price of everything, will
call a driver back, will telephone a restaurant, and have an interest in you not
writing about it. Ask them before you go out ("what should a taxi to X cost?")
and after something goes wrong. For anything involving a driver they arranged,
they're directly accountable.

---

## The embassy

For lost or confiscated passports, arrest, hospitalization, or being unable to
leave the country. **Not** for money — embassies do not recover funds or
intervene in commercial disputes. Knowing this in advance prevents an
unproductive day.

## Reporting it forward

Costs ten minutes and is the only part of this that helps anyone else: a
specific, dated post on r/<city> or r/travel, a TripAdvisor forum reply, a
Google Maps review naming the exact tactic and the exact numbers. Specificity is
what makes it useful — "taxi from CAI terminal 3 quoted 400 EGP, refused to use
the meter, June 2026" is searchable in a way that "beware of scams!" is not.

Those posts are exactly the sources `anchors.md` sends you to look for. The
recent, route-level report that saves the next traveler is the one somebody
bothered to write.
